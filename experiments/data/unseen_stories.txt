Harriet Vale kept the lighthouse on Corran Point for eleven years before the harbour board decided to automate it. In the first week of that last winter she wrote everything down: the colour of the sea at dawn, the number of gulls on the rail, the name of every boat that passed the headland. Most of the boats she knew. The Marigold went out at five with Tomas Reyes at the wheel, and it came back at noon with its hold full of mackerel. The Saint Brigid belonged to the Duffy brothers, who argued so loudly that she could hear them from the gallery. On the ninth day a boat she did not recognise anchored below the cliffs. It had no name painted on the bow, only a white number, and nobody came up on deck for two days. Harriet watched it through the brass telescope her father had left her. When the harbour master finally rowed out to ask questions, the boat was already gone, and the only thing floating where it had been was a wooden crate stamped with the same white number. Harriet wrote that number in her book three times, underlined it, and then went back to counting gulls.

Every summer the Lindqvist family drove north to the cabin by the lake, and every summer the same things went wrong. The car overheated near the bridge at Kallby. The key to the boathouse was missing until someone found it in the pocket of a coat that had hung by the door since the previous August. The water pump made a noise like an animal in pain. This year the youngest daughter, Saga, was nine, and she had decided to keep a list. She wrote down the overheating, the key, the pump, and the wasp nest under the porch. She wrote down that her grandfather told the same story about the pike he had caught in nineteen seventy-one, and that the pike grew longer every time he told it. On the fourth evening a storm came across the lake and the power failed. They ate by candlelight and played cards, and her grandfather told the pike story again. Saga checked her list. The pike had been sixty centimetres on the first night and was now almost a metre. She did not say anything. She only added a new line at the bottom of the page, under the wasps: the fish is still growing.

The night train from Vienna was delayed for three hours at the border, and the passengers in carriage seven began to talk to each other out of boredom. There was a violinist named Petra Novak travelling with an instrument case she would not let anyone touch. There was a retired judge who complained about the heating, and a young engineer called Marek who was going home for his father's funeral. Petra said the violin had belonged to her teacher, who had died in the spring and left it to her in a letter written on the back of a concert programme. The judge asked whether the letter would stand up in court. Marek said nothing for a long time. Around two in the morning the conductor came through and told them the delay was caused by a missing document for a crate in the freight wagon. Nobody paid much attention. At dawn the train finally moved, and the judge fell asleep. Marek asked Petra quietly what the concert programme on the back of the letter had been. She said it was the last concert her teacher ever gave, in a small church near the border, and that the programme listed a piece nobody had heard of. Marek looked out of the window at the station they were leaving. It was the same village.

When the bakery on Mill Street changed owners, the regular customers were suspicious of everything. The new owner, a tall woman named Ines Carvalho, kept the old sign and the old ovens, but she changed the bread. The rye was darker and the rolls were smaller. Mr. Abbott, who had bought two rolls every morning for twenty years, announced that he would never come back, and then came back the next day to tell her so again. Ines listened to him and gave him a roll for free. The following week she found an old notebook behind the flour sacks. It belonged to the previous owner, Walter, and it was full of recipes written in pencil, with notes in the margins about which customers liked what. On one page Walter had written that Mr. Abbott could not taste salt anymore and liked his rolls with extra butter brushed on top, but that he must never be told. Ines read the page twice. The next morning, when Mr. Abbott came in to complain, she handed him two rolls brushed with butter. He took a bite, stopped talking, and stood very still in the doorway. Then he paid for the rolls, which he had never done without an argument, and walked out without saying a word about the bread.

The expedition reached the glacier on the sixth day, a week behind schedule. Dr. Kaur, the leader, had planned to drill three ice cores near the northern edge, but the northern edge had moved. The maps were from twelve years earlier, and the ice had retreated almost a kilometre since then. The team argued about whether to drill at the old coordinates, which were now bare rock, or at the new edge, where the ice was thinner and younger. Sato, the youngest member, pointed out that the funding agency only cared about the coordinates in the proposal. Kaur said that drilling rock would tell them nothing. They drilled at the new edge. The first core came up cloudy, full of trapped dust. The second core was clear. The third core, taken closest to the old coordinates, contained a thin dark band about two metres down. Under the microscope the band turned out to be ash. Sato checked the dates in the proposal and then checked them again. The only eruption that matched was the one the old maps had been drawn to study, the one the expedition had originally been funded to find. They had found it by abandoning the plan that had been written to find it. Kaur laughed for the first time in six days.

For most of the season the football club in Hartwell lost every match, and the local paper stopped sending a reporter. The coach, Danny Muir, had played for the club himself thirty years earlier, in the only year it had ever won the county cup. He kept a photograph of that team in his office, and every Saturday he looked at it before the players arrived. In November a new goalkeeper joined the club, a student called Femi who had never played in goal before. He had volunteered because nobody else would. In his first match he let in five goals, and in his second he let in four. Muir did not replace him. He spent the evenings in the car park kicking balls at Femi until it was too dark to see. By February the club was losing by one goal instead of five. In March they drew. In April they won their first match in two years, and the paper sent a photographer. The photograph on the front page showed Femi holding the ball above his head. Muir cut it out and pinned it in his office next to the old team photograph. Visitors noticed that the goalkeeper in the old photograph was a younger Danny Muir, holding the ball in exactly the same way.

The archive had lost power twice that week, and the head archivist, Mrs. Oyelaran, was worried about the humidity. The documents in the basement were letters from the eighteen-nineties, written by a shipping clerk named Albert Crane to his sister in Glasgow. For most of a century nobody had read them. A graduate student, Theo, had been cataloguing them since September. Crane wrote about cargo, weather, and the prices of coal. He complained about his employer and about the food at his boarding house. Theo found the letters dull until he reached the winter of eighteen ninety-four, when Crane began to mention a ship that was not on any official list. He called it only the grey ship, and he wrote that its cargo was recorded under false names. In his final letter Crane told his sister he had copied the real manifest and hidden it where the company would never think to look. After that there were no more letters. Theo searched the archive for weeks. On the day the power failed for the third time, he sat in the dark with a torch and reread the first letter in the collection, the one about the boarding house. On the back, in pencil, someone had drawn a small grey ship.

The orchard at Brekke had been in the family for four generations, and every autumn the same argument started about which trees to cut down. Solveig wanted to keep the old Gravenstein trees near the road because her grandmother had planted them. Her cousin Arne wanted to replace them with a newer variety that resisted the scab. They argued in the kitchen, in the barn, and once, loudly, at a funeral. That year the harvest was poor. A late frost in May had killed most of the blossom, and only the trees on the upper slope produced anything worth selling. Arne counted the crates and said that the upper slope was all new trees, which proved his point. Solveig walked the rows in the evening with a notebook. She wrote down every tree that had fruit. When she compared her list with the planting records in the attic, she found that the upper slope was not new at all. The trees there had been grafted onto old Gravenstein roots forty years earlier, by her grandmother, who had never told anyone. Solveig showed Arne the records at breakfast. He read them twice, put down his coffee, and asked her which branches they should graft next spring.

The radio station at the edge of town broadcast for only six hours a day, and most of those hours belonged to a man called Desmond Achebe. He read the weather, the fishing reports, the obituaries, and the lost-and-found notices in the same slow, careful voice. People said they could set their clocks by him. In the spring a listener began sending letters to the station. The letters were unsigned, and each one asked Desmond to play a particular song at exactly ten past nine on a Thursday. The songs were old and hard to find. Desmond found them anyway, in the basement archive, on scratched records that had not been played in decades. He played them at ten past nine every Thursday without saying why. After eight weeks the letters stopped. Desmond was worried and mentioned it on air, which he had never done before. The next morning a woman arrived at the station with a box of records. She said her father had written the letters from the care home on the hill, where he listened every night, and that he had died on Wednesday. The records in the box were his. On the top one, in faded ink, someone had written the station's call sign and a date from nineteen sixty-two.

When the bridge over the Ness river closed for repairs, the ferry that had not run for thirty years was brought back into service. Its captain was a woman named Morag Finlay, who had learned to pilot it as a girl from her uncle. The ferry was slow and smelled of diesel, and at first the commuters complained about everything. They complained about the timetable, the benches, and the seagulls that followed the boat. Morag ignored them. She knew every sandbar in the river and every trick of the tide. By the second month something had changed. The commuters began to talk to each other. A nurse and a baker discovered they had gone to the same school. Two men who had lived on the same street for a decade finally learned each other's names. A retired teacher started bringing a flask of tea to share. When the council announced that the bridge would reopen early, a petition appeared asking for the ferry to continue. The council refused, citing the cost. On the final crossing Morag sounded the horn three times, which her uncle had always done on his last trip of the day. Half the passengers were crying. The next week the nurse and the baker opened a small cafe by the old landing.

The chess club met on Tuesday evenings in the back room of the public library, and for eleven years its strongest player had been an accountant named Viktor Hale. Nobody had beaten him in a club game since the winter he joined. Then a new member arrived, a thirteen-year-old girl called Amara who came with her grandmother and never said much. She lost her first three games against the weaker players, quickly and without complaint. Viktor paid no attention to her. In her second month she began to win. She beat the librarian, then the retired pilot, then the two brothers who had run the club before Viktor. In June she asked Viktor for a game. He agreed, a little amused, and played the opening he always played. Amara answered with a line he had never seen. It looked like a mistake at first, a pawn left undefended in the centre, but when he took it his position slowly came apart. After two hours he resigned. The room was silent. Amara's grandmother leaned over and said something quietly in another language. Later Viktor asked what she had said. Amara told him that her grandmother had played that exact line against his teacher, in a tournament in Odessa, and had lost.

The clockmaker's shop on Hollis Lane had been closed for a year when his nephew, Rafael, finally came to empty it. The shelves were covered in dust and the clocks had all stopped, each one at a different time. Rafael had not been close to his uncle, and he planned to sell everything to a dealer by the end of the week. On the first day he found the account books, which were neat and ordinary. On the second day he found a drawer full of letters from customers, thanking his uncle for repairs that had been done for free. On the third day he noticed something odd about the stopped clocks. He wrote down the time on each one and arranged the list in order around the room. The times were not random. Read from the door to the back wall, the hours and minutes formed a sequence of dates, and every date was a birthday in the family, including his own. The last clock, in the back corner, had stopped at a time that matched no birthday he knew. Rafael asked his mother about it that evening. She was quiet for a long while, and then she told him it was the day his uncle had opened the shop, fifty years before, the same day his own father had been born.
